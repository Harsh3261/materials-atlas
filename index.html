Equivalents — near is not identical

42CrMo (GB/T 3077) vs AISI 4140: NEAR — NOT INTERCHANGEABLE same Cr-Mo idea, different windows — 42CrMo Mn is 0.50–0.80%, 4140 Mn is 0.75–1.00%. A heat can pass one and fail the other on manganese alone. And the mechanical bases differ entirely: GB/T 3077 publishes Q&T reference-bar minimums; SAE J404 publishes no mechanicals at all.

40Cr vs AISI 5140: 40Cr's Cr window is 0.80–1.10% vs 5140's 0.70–0.90% — a 40Cr heat at 1.05% Cr is out of 5140 spec.

304 (ASTM A240) vs 1.4301 (EN 10088): NEAR — NOT INTERCHANGEABLE the canonical pair, but NEAR under the strict rule — the windows cross rather than nest: carbon is 0.07 max in both (the 0.08 figure sometimes quoted for A240 is the A276 bar limit, not A240), and the two standards set their remaining limits and mechanical minimums differently, so neither window contains the other. The EN minimum Rp0.2 (230 MPa, cold-rolled strip) is higher than A240's 205 MPa. Dual-certified material exists precisely because neither standard contains the other.

E355 / ST52.3 / “1026-class” honed tube: three different documents (EN 10305-1, withdrawn DIN 2391, ASTM A519) with three different property bases — spec minimums by delivery condition, legacy minimums, and typicals. Same barrel application, not the same guarantee.

20CrMo's EN counterpart is genuinely disputed — crosswalks list both 18CrMo4 (1.7243) and 25CrMo4 (1.7218), and neither reaches two-source confirmation, so the atlas asserts no EN designation for it (its AISI cross is likewise disputed). JIS SCM420 is the corroborated cross. C36000 vs CW603N is the standard free-cutting-brass pair, but Pb windows and temper designations differ — a cert must name the system it certifies to.

4340 (SAE J404) vs EN 34CrNiMo6 / 1.6582: XREF-ONLY Conventional cross-reference only — EN ISO 683-2 34CrNiMo6 (C 0.30–0.38, Cr 1.30–1.70, Ni 1.30–1.70) vs SAE J404 4340 (C 0.38–0.43, Cr 0.70–0.90, Ni 1.65–2.00): the Cr and Ni bands are disjoint. The JIS SNCM439 entry stays NEAR. XREF-ONLY — a conventional cross-reference whose composition bands do not even overlap; never substitute on it.

C95400 (ASTM B505) vs EN CC331G / CuAl10Fe2-C: NEAR — NOT INTERCHANGEABLE CC333G (Ni 4–6) is disjoint from C95400 (Ni max 1.5) and pairs with C95500; C95400's EN casting neighbour is CC331G, still NEAR — its Al/Fe windows run lower.

Substitution rule: a near-equivalent may be proposed by a supplier; it is qualified only when chemistry (all restricted elements), condition, form/thickness band, and mechanical basis are each checked against the specified standard — or the substitution is formally approved as a deviation.

1Quick rulesTen checks that find most cert problems.

  1. Compare a cert only against SPEC MIN rows. A value below a TYPICAL is not a rejection; a value at typical proves nothing about the minimum.

  2. No condition, no number. The same grade spans annealed 4140 at HB 187 to HTSR 4140 at HB 311+. Match the cert's condition before comparing anything.

  3. SAE J403 / J404 are chemistry only. A "1018 yield" always belongs to a condition and a source — never to the SAE grade itself.

  4. Tube: A513 DOM = minimums, A519 seamless = typicals. A519 strength only binds if the PO adds supplementary requirements.

  5. Match the delivery condition on EN tube. E355 +SR guarantees ReH ≥ 450 MPa; +N only ≥ 355 MPa.

  6. Check the size or thickness band. Plate, aluminium and Q&T bar minimums step down as the section grows.

  7. Know the yield method. Copper alloys (B505/B16) use 0.5% extension under load; steels use 0.2% offset. Don't compare across methods.

  8. Free-machining grades have rules. In 303/416 sulphur is a minimum (≥ 0.15%); 12L14 is a do-not-weld steel.

  9. Judge weldability by carbon equivalent. IIW CE ≤ 0.40 generally weldable · 0.40–0.60 preheat likely · > 0.60 procedure-qualification territory (screening only).

  10. "Equivalent" is not "interchangeable". 4140 Mn 0.75–1.00 vs GB/T 42CrMo 0.50–0.80 — a heat can pass one and fail the other.

3Heat treatmentHow the heat-treated condition sets a steel's properties.

Same grade, different heat treatment = a different material. Read the condition before you read any number.

The processes

Anneal +A · annealed

Heated into the austenite range, then cooled slowly in the furnace.

Hardness ↓↓ · machinability ↑

On a cert: A common supply state for 4130/4140 bar (annealed 4140 typically reads about HB 187–212, cold-finished at the upper end); 4140 is also widely stocked Q&T / pre-hard. A reading of HB 300+ on “annealed” bar means it isn't annealed.

Normalize +N · normalized

Heated a little higher than for annealing, then cooled in still air.

Finer, more uniform grain · a bit stronger than annealed

On a cert: Compare the A519 normalized rows against cold-worked. On EN 10305-1 tube, +N guarantees a lower yield than +SR (see the E355 example below).

Harden (quench) austenitize + quench

Heated above the upper line, then quenched in water, polymer or oil so it transforms to martensite.

Hardness ↑↑↑ · brittle until tempered

On a cert: The medium matters: the sourced rows show 4130 water-quenched, 4140/4340 oil-quenched. As-quenched steel is brittle; a hardened part should show a temper.

Temper +QT · Q&T

Reheated below the lower line after quenching and held. The temperature sets the final strength.

Hardness ↓ · toughness ↑ (hotter = softer, tougher)

On a cert: A Q&T row without a tempering temperature or a hardness is not a condition. The trend for 4140 is below.

Stress relieve +SR · SR · HTSR

Held below the lower line and cooled slowly, to relax machining, welding or straightening stress.

Little hardness change if kept below the last temper

On a cert: On Q&T parts it must stay below the tempering temperature or it softens them. HTSR = Q&T then stress relieved (bar trade, no standard).

Carburize case hardened

Carbon diffused into the surface at high temperature, then quenched and low-tempered.

Hard case · tough core

On a cert: 8620 and 20CrMo core properties are not case properties. Ask for case depth and surface hardness as separate results.

Nitride gas / plasma nitrided

Nitrogen diffused into the surface at a lower temperature, with no quench, usually on Q&T steel.

Thin, very hard case · minimal distortion

On a cert: The nitriding temperature must stay below the part's tempering temperature, or the core softens. Case depth and surface hardness are separate results.

Induction harden IH · induction hardened

Only the surface layer is heated by induction and quenched, as on induction-hardened chrome piston rod.

Hard surface band · core unchanged

On a cert: Surface hardness and effective case depth are separate results from the core's tensile and yield.

Age / precipitation harden H900 · H1150 · T6

Solution treated, then aged at a set temperature so fine precipitates form.

Strength ↑ · the aging temperature sets the balance

On a cert: 17-4 PH: the H-number is the aging temperature in °F (H900 hard, H1150 tough). Aluminium T6 = solution treated + artificially aged.

Common heat-treatment errors on certs

Example: E355 tube EN 10305-1

The delivery condition changes the guarantee. +N guarantees ReH ≥ 355 MPa; +SR, the most common honed-barrel condition, guarantees ≥ 450 MPa.

On a cert: Many online datasheets quote the +N figure (ReH ≥ 355 MPa), about 20 % below the +SR minimum (≥ 450 MPa). Match the cert's delivery condition to the row before comparing.

Flame-cut edges oxy-fuel · plasma

Cutting leaves a heat-affected zone: a hardened, sometimes micro-cracked edge on hardenable grades, and a softened band on Q&T plate (A514 class) where the cut over-tempers the edge. Higher carbon equivalent and thicker plate both make the HAZ harder.

On a cert: Never report a flame-cut edge's hardness as the plate's. Check the PO or drawing for edge requirements (grind or machine back the HAZ for fatigue or weld prep).

4Conditions glossary16 condition words from certs and POs — what each means and what to check.

The floor check: annealed 4140 reads ≈ HB 187–197; HTSR 4140 reads HB 311–331 typical. No overlap — a reading near HB 300+ on bar sold as annealed means it isn't annealed, whatever the paperwork says.

As-rolled / hot rolled (HR)

Used straight off the mill, no heat treatment after rolling.

Why it matters: Properties vary with section size and cooling. Note: 4140 is normally sold hot rolled and annealed (≈ HB 187–197); true as-rolled 4140 is harder and less predictable — the cert must say which.

HRR

Hot rolled round — a hot-rolled round bar. It is a product-form + as-rolled label, not a heat treatment.

Why it matters: A common labelling error: HRR is soft stock. Never attach Q&T numbers to it — if the hardness reads HB 300+, it is not plain HRR.

Annealed (+A)

Heated and slow-cooled to soften and homogenise.

Why it matters: Softest, most machinable condition; 4140 annealed ≈ HB 187. The baseline condition for most bar purchasing.

Normalised (+N)

Heated above the transformation range, air-cooled.

Why it matters: Finer, more uniform grain than as-rolled; slightly harder/stronger than annealed.

Stress relieved (+SR)

Held below the transformation temperature (typically ~500–650 °C for steels) and cooled — relieves internal stress without changing hardness much.

Why it matters: Bought when machining stability matters. In ASTM A519, “SR” is a defined suffix condition. DOM tube (A513) is sold either as-drawn or stress relieved — the cert must say which.

HTSR

Heat treated and stress relieved — quenched & tempered first, then stress relieved. A trade condition (North American bar trade), not a standard temper.

Why it matters: Pre-hard stock around HB 300+ (4140: HB 311–331 typical). Ready to machine; no further hardening planned.

Pre-hardened / PH (bar trade)

Supplied already hardened & tempered to a working hardness (commonly 28–34 HRC / ~270–330 HB) — the same idea as HTSR.

Why it matters: Bought to skip in-house heat treatment. Not to confuse with PH = precipitation hardening for stainless grades like 17-4 PH.

Quenched & tempered (+QT)

Hardened by quenching, then tempered to the required strength. The only condition in which most alloy-steel standards (EN ISO 683, AS 1444 U/V/W…) quote their big numbers.

Why it matters: Strength depends on the ruling section — big bars quench slower and test softer. Always check which section size a Q&T number belongs to.

Heat treated (HT) — copper castings

In ASTM B505, the HT suffix means solution heat treated (and annealed/aged per the standard's Table 2 cycles) — the bronze equivalent of a Q&T row.

Why it matters: Separate minimums from as-cast; never average the two.

Temper annealed

The specific B505 cycle for C95800 (≈1200–1300 °F, 6 h, air cool) that stabilises the microstructure for seawater service.

Why it matters: A C95800 cert for marine service should show it — B505 §6.1 makes it mandatory for seawater.

TQ30

The B505 temper designation for quenched + tempered C95520 — the only condition C95520 is supplied in.

Why it matters: If a C95520 cert shows any other condition, stop and ask.

Aged (e.g. 17-4 PH H900/H1150)

Precipitation hardening: solution treated, then aged at a stated temperature; the H-number is the aging temperature in °F.

Why it matters: H1150 (1150 °F) is the soft, tough, SCC-resistant end; H900 the hard end. Properties differ widely by H-condition — always carry the suffix.

As-cast

Used straight from the mould (sand or continuous cast), no heat treatment.

Why it matters: The default B505 condition; HT variants are separate rows with separate minimums.

Cold worked / cold drawn (CW, CD)

Strength raised by cold deformation rather than heat treatment.

Why it matters: Higher strength, lower ductility; A519 CW is a defined condition.

DOM (drawn over mandrel)

Welded tube cold-drawn over a mandrel for tight tolerances — ASTM A513 Type 5.

Why it matters: A513 gives minimums, supplied as-drawn or stress relieved (different minimums — the cert must say which). Don't confuse it with seamless mechanical tube (ASTM A519), whose table is typicals.

HRAP

Hot rolled, annealed & pickled — the standard stainless plate finish (finish designations per ASTM A480).

Why it matters: The default supply condition behind this atlas's A240 rows.

Glossary basis: ASTM A519 condition suffix definitions; ASTM B505 §6/Table 2 (HT, temper anneal, TQ30); EN designation practice (+A/+N/+QT); AS 1444 condition letters; bar-trade HTSR/PH usage per Vanguard Steel and Richmond Steel. HTSR has NO governing standard — no HTSR row anywhere can be a spec minimum.

Material science for QA

The background behind the numbers: what's inside steel, how it's tested, why it hardens, welds, corrodes and fails, and how it's inspected. Heat treatment is lesson 3 above.

7Inside steelPhases, the iron–carbon diagram, and why carbon sets hardness.

Steel is iron with up to about 2 % carbon. What the carbon does depends on which phase it is sitting in, and the phase depends on temperature and cooling rate.

Phases and structures

Ferrite (α) BCC

Body-centred cubic iron. Soft, ductile and magnetic. Dissolves almost no carbon (max 0.022 % at 727 °C).

Why it matters: Low-carbon grades such as 1018 are mostly ferrite, which is why they weld and form easily.

Austenite (γ) FCC

Face-centred cubic iron, stable above the A1/A3 lines in carbon steel. Dissolves up to 2.14 % carbon and is non-magnetic.

Why it matters: Every hardening treatment starts by turning the steel into austenite. 304/316 stainless keep austenite at room temperature because of their nickel.

Cementite Fe₃C

Iron carbide, 6.67 % carbon. Very hard and brittle.

Why it matters: 2.14 % carbon is the textbook boundary between steel and cast iron.

Pearlite α + Fe₃C

Fine alternating plates of ferrite and cementite that form when austenite cools slowly through A1 (727 °C). A 0.76 % C steel is all pearlite.

Why it matters: Annealed and normalized medium-carbon bar (1045, 4140 +A/+N) is ferrite plus pearlite. That is the soft, machinable state.

Martensite BCT

Forms when austenite is cooled too fast for carbon to escape. The trapped carbon distorts the lattice, so it is very hard and brittle until tempered.

Why it matters: Its hardness is set mainly by carbon content, not by the alloying. Alloying sets how deep it forms (see Hardenability).

Bainite

Forms at cooling rates between those that make pearlite and martensite. Strong and tough.

Why it matters: Thick Q&T sections often have a bainite core instead of full martensite, which is one reason big bars test softer.

Floor checks that come from this

The magnet test

Ferritic, martensitic and duplex steels are magnetic; annealed 304/316 is essentially not.

On the floor: Cold-worked 304 can turn partly magnetic (strain-induced martensite), so a slightly magnetic 304 bushing is not proof of a mix-up. Use chemistry (PMI) to settle it.

Grain size ASTM E112

Reported as an ASTM grain size number; a higher number means finer grains.

On a cert: Fine grain (often ASTM 5 or finer) improves toughness. Some specs and POs require it to be reported on the cert.

Basis: Fe–Fe₃C diagram values per Callister, Materials Science and Engineering (eutectoid 727 °C / 0.76 % C; max C 0.022 % in ferrite, 2.14 % in austenite; Fe₃C 6.67 % C). Older texts give 723 °C / 0.8 %. ASTM E112 grain size.

8Mechanical testingWhat each number on a cert measures.

Tensile test

Yield strength Rp0.2 · ReH

The stress where permanent deformation starts. Most steels use the 0.2 % offset method; steels with a sharp yield point report the upper yield (ReH).

On a cert: Copper alloys (B505/B16) use 0.5 % extension under load, so their yield is not comparable to a steel's 0.2 % offset value.

Tensile strength Rm · UTS

The highest stress reached, divided by the original area.

On a cert: Hardness correlates with tensile strength in steel (E140 / ISO 18265), but it is an estimate, never a replacement for a required tensile test.

Elongation A · %El

Plastic stretch at fracture, measured over a marked gauge length.

On a cert: Gauge length changes the number: ASTM E8 round specimens use 4×D; E8M and ISO 6892-1 use 5×D (5.65√S₀). The longer gauge reads a lower %, so a 4D value and a 5D value aren't directly comparable.

Reduction of area Z · RA

How much the cross-section shrank at the break.

On a cert: A low RA on a ductile grade points to inclusions or hydrogen. Some Q&T and bolting specs set a minimum.

Where the specimen came from ASTM A370

Steel products are tested per ASTM A370. For bar, the specimen location (e.g. mid-radius) and direction (longitudinal vs transverse) are defined.

On a cert: Transverse values are lower than longitudinal ones. Check that the cert's specimen matches what the spec calls for.

Hardness

Brinell HBW · ASTM E10

A 10 mm tungsten-carbide ball at 3000 kgf for steel, held 10–15 s; the impression diameter is measured. Valid up to 650 HBW.

On a cert: A big impression averages the structure, so it suits bar and castings. Current E10 allows only the carbide ball (HBW); older HBS steel-ball values read differently.

Rockwell C / B HRC · HRB · ASTM E18

10 kgf preload, then HRC: 120° diamond cone at 150 kgf total; HRB: 1/16 in ball at 100 kgf total. Depth of penetration is measured.

On a cert: Use each scale in its range (HRC 20–70, HRB up to 100). A reading of HRC below 20 or HRB above 100 belongs on the other scale. Rockwell C is not suitable on chrome plating.

Vickers / micro HV · ASTM E92 / E384

A diamond pyramid at a chosen load; micro-loads read thin layers.

On a cert: The method for case depth on carburized, nitrided or induction-hardened parts, and for thin plating.

Conversions ASTM E140 · ISO 18265

Tables converting between scales and to approximate tensile strength.

On a cert: Conversions are approximate and material-specific. Report the scale you actually tested, and mark any conversion as converted.

Impact

Charpy V-notch CVN · ASTM E23

A 10 × 10 × 55 mm bar with a 2 mm, 45° V-notch, broken by a pendulum. Reports absorbed energy (J, or ft·lbf on many US certs) at a stated temperature.

On a cert: A CVN value without its test temperature means nothing. BCC steels (carbon, low-alloy, ferritic) have a ductile-to-brittle transition as temperature drops; FCC austenitic stainless does not.

Basis: ASTM A370, E8/E8M, ISO 6892-1, E10, E18 (via NIST SP 960-5), E92, E384, E140, ISO 18265, E23.

9HardenabilityHow deep a steel hardens, which is not the same as how hard.

Hardness is how hard the steel gets, and that mainly depends on carbon. Hardenability is how deep it hardens in a real section, and that mainly depends on alloying (Cr, Mo, Ni, Mn, B).

Jominy end-quench test ASTM A255

A 1 in × 4 in bar is austenitized, then water-sprayed on one end only. Hardness is read along the bar at 1/16 in steps.

On a cert: Close to the quenched end = what a thin section reaches. Further along = what the middle of a thick section reaches.

H-steels 4140H · SAE J1268

Grades with an "H" suffix are bought to a guaranteed Jominy band instead of tight chemistry alone.

On a cert: Bars bought to end-quench limits (ASTM A304). When through-hardening a heavy section matters, the H-grade buys a predictable result.

Ruling section (mass effect)

Bigger sections cool slower in the quench, so their core hardens less.

On a cert: Q&T minimums step down as section size grows (EN ISO 683-2, GB/T 3077's 25 mm test bar). A cert tested on a small coupon doesn't prove a large bar's core.

Why 4140 over 1045

Similar carbon, so a similar maximum hardness, but 4140's Cr-Mo lets it harden through much larger sections in oil.

Why it matters: Plain-carbon 1045 needs a water quench for depth, which raises distortion and quench-crack risk.

Basis: ASTM A255 (Jominy end-quench, DI calculation), ASTM A304 and SAE J1268 (H-steels), EMJ Mechanical Properties and Hardenability (mass-effect data). The Jominy curves are schematic; the size table is data.

10Welding and carbon equivalentWhy some steels crack after welding, and what prevents it.

Carbon equivalent IIW CE

CE = C + Mn/6 + (Cr + Mo + V)/5 + (Ni + Cu)/15, in wt %.

On a cert: TWI guidance: below 0.40 readily weldable · 0.40–0.50 preheat on thicker sections · above 0.50 preheat, low-hydrogen process and controlled cooling. Check a Cert › Step 2 calculates CE from the cert chemistry (its bands are a coarser screen).

Hydrogen (cold) cracking

Needs all three together: hydrogen in the weld, a hard (martensitic) HAZ, and tensile stress. It can appear hours after welding.

On a cert: That's why critical welds are inspected after a delay, not straight away.

Preheat and interpass

Slows cooling so the HAZ is softer, and lets hydrogen escape.

On a cert: Higher CE, thicker sections and more restraint all need more preheat; keep interpass near preheat. The WPS or code sets the number (e.g. AWS D1.1, EN 1011-2).

Low-hydrogen consumables E7018 · H4 / H8

Low-hydrogen electrodes, kept dry, with a diffusible-hydrogen designator (e.g. H4 = ≤ 4 mL/100 g).

On a cert: On 4140 and other high-CE steels, low-hydrogen practice is assumed, not optional.

4140 and other hardenable steels

Typical 41xx preheat rises with thickness: about 300–400 °F at 3/16–1/2 in, 400–500 °F at 1/2–1 in, 600–800 °F above 1 in (Hobart guidance), often with a post-weld bake or temper.

On a cert: Welding Q&T or HTSR bar softens the HAZ next to the weld and hardens the weld line itself. Check the drawing before anyone welds it.

Don't-weld grades 12L14 · 1144 · 303

Lead and high sulphur cause hot cracking and porosity.

On a cert: Free-machining grades are not for welded joints.

Heat-affected zone HAZ

Base metal next to the weld that was heated but not melted. Its hardness and toughness change.

On a cert: Weld procedure qualification (e.g. ASME IX, AWS D1.1) is what proves the HAZ is acceptable.

Basis: IIW carbon equivalent; TWI Job Knowledge 45/46 (hydrogen cracking, CE guidance); AWS D1.1 Annex B; AWS A5.1 (E7018 H4); Hobart Brothers 41xx preheat guidance; ASME Section IX.

11Corrosion and coatingsHow steel rusts, how stainless resists it, and what coatings add.

Galvanic corrosion

Two metals in contact in an electrolyte: the less noble one (anode) corrodes faster.

On a cert: A small anode against a large cathode is the worst case, e.g. zinc-plated screws in stainless sheet.

Why stainless resists rust ≥ 10.5 % Cr

Chromium forms a thin passive oxide film that heals itself in air.

On a cert: Iron contamination from carbon-steel tools can rust on a stainless surface. Passivation (ASTM A967 / A380) removes it.

Pitting and chlorides PREN

Chlorides break the passive film locally. PREN = Cr + 3.3·Mo + 16·N ranks resistance.

On a cert: 316's molybdenum gives it a higher PREN than 304, so it suits salt and coolant exposure better.

Hard chrome (piston rod) engineering chrome

Electroplated engineering chromium (ASTM B650, AMS 2460, ISO 6158). Rod-grade chrome is typically ≥ 20 µm thick, ≥ 850 HV0.1 and Ra ≤ 0.2 µm, over a Q&T or induction-hardened base (Ovako Cromax data).

On a cert: Ask for plating thickness and Vickers hardness (Rockwell C is not valid on chrome). Conventional rod-grade hard chrome is micro-cracked, so corrosion protection depends on thickness and base finish.

Electroplating and hydrogen bake

Plating and acid cleaning put hydrogen into the steel. High-strength parts need a relief bake soon after plating.

On a cert: ASTM F1941: bake required above 39 HRC (≥ 14 h at 190–220 °C; 24 h above 44 HRC). ISO 4042: bake required above 390 HV. A plated high-hardness part should show the bake on the cert.

Zinc plating ASTM B633 · ISO 4042

Sacrificial coating. Minimum thickness by service condition: SC1 5 µm · SC2 8 µm · SC3 12 µm · SC4 25 µm, plus a finish type (I–VI). Not for steel above 1700 MPa / 46 HRC.

On a cert: The SC/type on the cert should match the drawing.

Nitriding gas · plasma

Nitrogen diffused in at about 495–565 °C (gas) or 400–590 °C (plasma), no quench. Thin hard case, usually under 0.5 mm, with a brittle compound ("white") layer on top.

On a cert: The part is Q&T first and tempered at least 30–50 °C above the nitriding temperature, so the core keeps its properties. For gas nitriding, AMS 2759/10 classes limit the white layer; ion/plasma nitriding is AMS 2759/8.

Basis: ASTM A967, A380, B650, B633, F1941; ISO 4042, ISO 6158; AMS 2460, AMS 2759/8, AMS 2759/10; Ovako Cromax datasheet; TWI and NMFRC (hard chrome); Total Materia and Bodycote (nitriding); SSINA.

12How parts failReading a broken part, and what each failure mode needs.

Fatigue

Repeated stress below yield grows a crack from a stress raiser (sharp corner, thread root, scratch, inclusion) until what's left breaks. For polished steel test specimens the endurance limit is roughly half the tensile strength (up to about 1400 MPa UTS; about 700 MPa above that). Real parts are lower once surface finish, size and notches count.

On a broken part: Fracture face: smooth, beach-marked zone pointing back to the origin, then a rough final-overload zone. Surface finish and fillet radius matter as much as strength.

Ductile overload

The part was loaded beyond its strength in one go.

On a broken part: Necking, a cup-and-cone or slanted fibrous surface, visible deformation.

Brittle fracture

Fast crack with little deformation, made likelier by low temperature, high hardness, notches and thick sections.

On a broken part: Flat, bright, granular surface; chevron marks point back to the origin.

Hydrogen embrittlement

Hydrogen from plating, pickling or welding makes high-strength steel (roughly above 30–39 HRC, depending on the source) crack under sustained stress, often hours or days later.

On a broken part: Delayed cracking of hard fasteners or springs after plating. Check for the post-plating bake.

Stress corrosion cracking SCC

Needs a susceptible alloy, a specific environment and tensile stress together. Classic case: austenitic stainless in chlorides, rare below 60 °C unless chlorides concentrate by evaporation.

On a broken part: Branching cracks, often with little general corrosion.

Wear and galling

Material lost or torn between sliding surfaces. Stainless on stainless galls easily.

On the floor: Use dissimilar materials or coatings for threads and sliding fits.

Preserve the evidence: don't fit broken halves back together, don't clean the fracture face with a wire brush, and keep it dry. The fracture face is the root-cause record.

Basis: Shigley's Mechanical Engineering Design (endurance limit); TMS failure-analysis course notes (fatigue, ductile/brittle, hydrogen); SSINA (chloride SCC); ASTM F1941.

3 · Hardness, adhesion, corrosion

  • B578 coating microhardness (Knoop): taken on a cross-section, not the surface; readings made on the plated surface aren't reportable. The coating must be at least 25 µm for hard coatings (nickel, cobalt, iron, hard gold) and 38 µm for soft ones (gold, copper, silver). A thin SC1 or SC2 electroless nickel can't be hardness tested on the part, so use a thicker test panel run with the parts.
  • B571 adhesion: qualitative tests (bend, file, grind/saw, heat-quench, impact, peel, push, scribe and others), each suited to certain coatings. For acceptance, the method must be specified. If more than one test is used, failing any one is a reject. If parts are baked or heat treated after plating, test adhesion after that treatment too.
  • B117 salt spray: the fog test behind the hours in B633, MIL-PRF-8625 and others. It's a process check against the spec's hours, and applies only if the order calls for it.
  • B602 sampling: attribute sampling plans for coating inspection. MIL-PRF-8625 Amd 2 requires MIL-STD-1916 instead.

4 · Traps that catch good inspectors

  • Gauges read the total, not the layers. Magnetic gauges measure the combined thickness of every layer down to the base metal. A nickel + chrome or nickel + cobalt rod needs a cross-section for each layer.
  • Calibrate on the same base metal. B499 requires the calibration base to have the same magnetic properties as the part, and B244 the same electrical conductivity. Zeroing on mild steel and measuring an alloy-steel rod, or on 6061 and measuring 7075, builds in an error.
  • Electroless nickel changes after heat treatment. High-phosphorus EN is non-magnetic enough for B499 only before heat treatment, so measure before any Class 2 hardening bake.
  • XRF and beta give mass, not microns. The microns on the screen assume a density. For alloys like zinc-nickel or cobalt-phosphorus, check the instrument's calibration is set up for that alloy.
  • A cross-section is only as good as its prep. At ±0.8 µm, B487 is already a meaningful share of a 5 µm SC1 zinc, so a poor section on thin coatings can decide accept or reject.

Quick rule: zinc and chrome on steel → B499. Nickel → B530. Anodize → B244. Cobalt, or anything with two layers → B487 cross-section (or B568 XRF).
